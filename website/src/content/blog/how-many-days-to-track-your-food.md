Nearly every post on this blog lands on the same advice: read the week, not the day. I've said it about [upper limits](/blog/nutrient-upper-limits), about [absorption](/blog/bioavailability-what-you-actually-absorb), about [where the data comes from](/blog/where-the-numbers-come-from), and last week about [what 100% of the RDA means](/blog/what-100-percent-of-the-rda-means).

I never checked whether a week is enough.

Researchers have been working on that question since at least the 1980s, and their answer depends on what you want the log to tell you. "How many days" is really two questions, and the answers are far apart.

## One day is a sample

Nutrition research has a name for the thing you care about: usual intake. The National Cancer Institute's Dietary Assessment Primer defines it as long-term average daily intake. That average is what the reference values are meant to be compared with, not any single day.

A single day is a noisy sample of it. The Primer says one day, "or even a few, does not represent a person's average daily intake."

How noisy depends on the nutrient. The Institute of Medicine's 2000 report on using the reference values to assess diets includes a table of day-to-day variation, built from a national survey that collected two days of intake per person. Here is the typical swing from one day to the next, as a percentage of average intake, for men aged 19 to 50:

| Nutrient | Day-to-day variation |
| --- | --- |
| Calories | 34% |
| Potassium | 36% |
| Magnesium | 38% |
| Sodium | 43% |
| Iron | 51% |
| Calcium | 54% |
| Folate | 61% |
| Zinc | 63% |
| Vitamin C | 92% |
| Vitamin A | 115% |
| Vitamin B12 | 212% |

*Within-person coefficient of variation, from US survey data collected 1994 to 1996. Food only, no supplements.*

Women's values are within a few points of these, except vitamin A (152%) and B12 (294%), which swing even more.

The order isn't random. Potassium and magnesium are spread thinly across almost everything you eat, so one day looks a lot like the next. Vitamin A and B12 are different. A few foods carry far more of them than anything else, and most people eat those foods only now and then. On the NIH's table of selected vitamin A sources, a 3 oz serving of beef liver has 6,582 µg. The next highest food, a baked sweet potato, has 1,403.

The NCI Primer makes a related point. For many nutrients, and especially for ones eaten episodically rather than daily, a person varies more from day to day than people vary from each other.

## Question one: where is my diet thin?

For this one, a few days go a long way.

In 2025, researchers at EPFL in Switzerland published an analysis of 958 adults who logged their meals in a phone app, mostly by photo, for two to four weeks. That came to more than 315,000 meals. They asked how many days it takes before a short stretch of logging reliably reflects a person's usual intake.

Most macronutrients got there in two to three days. Micronutrients generally took three to four. Their conclusion was that three to four days, ideally not consecutive and including at least one weekend day, are enough for most nutrients.

"Reliable" has a specific meaning there. The bar was a correlation of about 0.8 between the short-run average and the true one. A correlation that high means people mostly keep their relative positions: someone who runs low on magnesium over the whole period also looks low after three or four days. It doesn't mean their number is right.

That's a fine standard for finding gaps, because a big gap doesn't need precision. **Three days can't tell 35% from 45%. It can tell 35% from 100%.**

Two caveats. It's one cohort in one country. And nobody logged for more than four weeks, so foods eaten less often than that are outside what the study could see.

## Question two: what is my number?

This one is much harder.

In a study published in 1987, the USDA's human nutrition research center in Beltsville had 29 people record everything they ate for 365 days straight. The researchers then asked how many days of records it takes for one person's average to land within 10% of their true full-year average, 95% of the time.

For calories the answer averaged 31 days. For vitamin A it averaged 433, which is longer than the study ran.

The reason is arithmetic. Random error shrinks with the square root of the number of days, so cutting it in half takes four times as many days.

Here's what that means for a week. Take magnesium, with its 38% day-to-day swing. By my arithmetic, a seven-day average lands within about 28% of your long-run average, 95% of the time. A bar that reads 90% for the week is consistent with a true figure anywhere from about 70% to 125%.

Last week's post laid out three zones for reading a bar: at or above the RDA, below the average requirement, and in between. For magnesium the average requirement sits at 83% of the RDA. One week at 90% can't tell you which zone you're in.

That calculation assumes each day is independent and the swings are roughly bell-shaped. The IOM table warns that once the variation passes 60 to 70%, daily intakes are too skewed for the method to be reliable. Vitamin C, vitamin A and B12 are far past that line, so I won't print a margin for them. Any figure I gave would be false precision.

## A week with liver in it

Go back to [my two carnivore days](/blog/a-carnivore-day-in-micronutrients). Day 1, the muscle-meat day, came in at 44% of the vitamin A target. A 3 oz serving of liver has about 6,500 µg, roughly seven times the target.

Suppose someone eats Day 1 every day and adds that serving of liver every Saturday. Their real average is about 147% of the target.

Now log three days. Tuesday through Thursday shows 44%. Friday through Sunday shows 285%. It's the same person eating the same diet, and both readings are wrong.

For a nutrient that arrives in occasional large doses, a full week is the smallest window that means anything, and only when the dose repeats every week. If liver is an every-other-week food, no single week is right either.

## Which days you pick

Weekends are different. In US survey data from the mid-1990s, people ate 82 more calories per day from Friday through Sunday than from Monday through Thursday. For adults aged 19 to 50 the difference was 115. In the Swiss app data, Saturdays ran 371 calories above Mondays.

So three weekdays is a biased sample. Spread the days out, too. Tuesday's leftovers are Wednesday's lunch, so back-to-back days tell you less than days spread across a couple of weeks.

## What more days can't fix

More days shrink random noise. They do nothing about error that points the same way every day.

**Logging changes eating.** The NCI calls this reactivity. People who know they're being measured may eat different foods or different amounts, sometimes just to make the logging simpler. The record can be accurate for those days and still not be your usual diet.

**People under-report.** In one study the NCI cites, 450 postmenopausal women kept food records while their intake was checked against biomarkers. The records came in about 20% low on energy and about 4% low on protein.

**Skipped days aren't neutral.** The weekly watch in Nutrient Logger averages your intake across the days you logged. If the days you skip are the restaurant days, the average describes your home cooking.

## What I'd do with this

**To find your gaps, log three or four days.** Spread them out and include a weekend day. Believe the big gaps. Hold the near-misses loosely.

**To judge a number, think in weeks and months.** For steady nutrients like magnesium and potassium, a week gets you a rough figure and a month gets you a decent one.

**Know which bars are spiky.** For vitamin A, B12 and vitamin C, one food on one day can outweigh the rest of the week. Read those over the longest stretch you have.

Nutrient Logger names your lowest nutrients after your first full day of logging. Treat that as a first guess. One day is enough to point at something and not enough to confirm it.

I've been telling you to read the week and not the day, and I still think that's right. The week is where the numbers start to mean something. For some of them it's only the start.
